# Writing craft

Rules any skilled writer follows in reader-facing prose ([defined in `prose-files.md`](./agents/prose-files.md#terms)), whatever their voice; it sits beside the Voice sheet, [`writing-voice.md`](./writing-voice.md), and academic work layers [`academic-writing-style.md`](./academic-writing-style.md) on both.

A rule belongs here when a good writer with a different voice would still follow it.

One line per rule. The examples and their sources are one file away. Open a reasoning file only when you're changing the rule, or the rule's line doesn't decide your case.

## Terms

- **Garden path**: a sentence whose first words invite one parse that a later word overturns, so the reader has to back up and reread.
- **Curse of knowledge**: the writer's difficulty imagining what the reader doesn't already know, which leaves terms undefined and steps skipped.
- **Given before new**: the order that puts what the reader already knows early in a sentence and the new information at its end.
- **Heavy phrase**: a phrase that is long or has phrases nested inside it.
- **Noun pile**: nouns stacked as modifiers before a head noun.
- **Center-embedding**: a clause nested in the middle of another, rather than at its start or end.
- **Metaconcept**: a word for a kind of idea ("issue", "level", "framework") standing where the idea itself should be.

## Precedence

- **A Craft rule wins over a Voice habit only when the habit makes the reader misread or reread a sentence; matters of taste stay with the habit.**

## Sentences

- **Open a sentence on what the reader already has (the last sentence's subject, a figure just given) and end it on the new information.**
- **Put a sentence's longest, most complex phrase at its end, and keep a phrase that opens it short.**
- **Use the passive when it lets a sentence open on its topic or on what the reader already has, or moves a long doer to the end.**
- **When a sentence's opening words invite a reading that a later word overturns, reword it: swap the ambiguous word ("once" for "when"), add a word that signals the structure, or reorder.**
- **Keep "that" after a verb such as "found" or "confirmed", and keep "which", "who" or "the", wherever dropping it lets the next noun read as the start of the wrong phrase.**
- **Place a modifier so that the nearest phrase it could attach to is the one it modifies; when a closer phrase could take it, move it.**
- **Don't put a clause in the middle of another, between a subject and its verb or between "and" and what it joins; set it beside the clause or give it its own sentence.** A short aside in paired dashes is fine.
- **Make the items joined by "and", "or", "both… and" or "not only… but also" the same kind of phrase, each working alone with what precedes it; put "both", "either" or "not only" just before the first item.**
- **Give parallel points the same sentence shape, in the same order (cause then effect, say), rather than varying it for variety's sake.**
- **Unpack nouns stacked before a noun into a phrase with a preposition ("the gate on HELOC funding", not "the HELOC funding gate").**

## Words

- **Name the thing itself, not a word for the kind of thing it is ("issue", "level", "approach", "framework", "process", "perspective", "strategy"): "rent may not cover the mortgage", not "there are affordability issues".**
- **Call a thing by what it is, with the detail that lets the reader picture it ("the 1950s boiler", "a three-bedroom unit"), not by the part it plays in your work ("the asset", "the subject property", "the test conditions").**
- **When a piece names something, build the name from plain words that say what it is ("the backup HELOC"), never a letter, a number or an initialism coined for the piece ("Option B", "Scenario 2", "BH").** A table or figure keeps its number.
- **The first time a term of the trade appears, add a few plain words saying what it is, unless every reader of the piece uses it daily.**
- **When everyday words say exactly what a term of the trade says, use them ("rent", not "gross scheduled income").**
- **Put quotation marks around words only to quote someone, to cite a term rather than use it, or to show you reject another's use of it; never to hold your own phrasing at arm's length.**
- **Never let two idioms whose pictures clash share a sentence.**

## Explaining

- **Choose a comparison that works the way the idea it explains works, not one that only looks like it.**
- **When one example could be read as showing the wrong feature, add a second that shares only the feature you mean.**
- **Don't tell the reader a topic is complex, contested or hard to define; define it, or show where the disagreement lies.**

## Signposts

- **Point back or ahead through the reader ("as we saw", "let's turn to") or with a question the next paragraphs answer, never by making the text the actor ("The previous section showed", "This section examines").** A table or figure may still "list" or "show".
